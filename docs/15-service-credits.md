# 15. Service credits: can labour back a currency?

**Short answer: partly, and not for the service you most want it for.**

Service credits work for abundant, low-training, locally-supplied labour.
They fail precisely where the grievance is sharpest — scarce specialists —
and they fail for a structural reason that no amount of good design removes.

This section takes the grievance seriously first, because much of it is
correct.

---

## 15.1 The grievance, checked against the numbers

> *"You have to pay someone's net worth to save him. Then they go into debt
> while the doctor is fine and got his millions."*

**This describes something real and enormous.** WHO's Africa region reports
that out-of-pocket health payments push over **150 million people into or
deeper into poverty**, and that Africa now accounts for **half of everyone
impoverished globally by health costs** — while the average African spends
only about **US$35 a year** on health. Catastrophic health spending is not an
edge case; it is the normal experience of illness for a large share of
humanity.

So the moral premise is not in dispute. Illness is a wealth-destroying event
for the patient and an income event for the provider, and the person is
usually least able to pay at the moment they must.

### On "the equipment excuse"

You said you disagree with the equipment justification, and suspect it is a
recycled excuse. **The data largely supports you, with one important
correction.**

| Input | Share of delivered cost |
|---|---|
| Practitioner labour | 45% |
| Support labour | 12% |
| **Consumables and drugs** | **25%** |
| **Equipment amortisation** | **8%** |
| Facility and utilities | 7% |
| Administration | 3% |

US hospital benchmarks put labour at **56–60%** of expenses and supplies plus
drugs around **21–22%**; capital equipment is a small slice of the total.

**Your instinct is right:** an X-ray machine is bought once and used for
fifteen years. Capital equipment at ~8% cannot by itself justify a bill that
bankrupts a family. Anyone citing "the equipment" as the whole explanation is
either confused or evading.

**The correction that matters:** the large non-labour cost is not equipment,
it is **consumables** — gloves, sutures, reagents, antibiotics, oxygen, test
strips. These are bought *again for every patient*, they are usually
imported, and they must be paid for in hard currency. That is 25% of cost
that no labour credit can ever touch.

**Combined: about 60% of the cost of care is labour and could in principle be
paid in service credits. About 40% cannot be paid in anything except real
goods or foreign exchange.** That ratio is the ceiling on this entire idea,
and it should be stated before anything else.

## 15.2 The structural problem: a service credit is not a commodity note

This is the difference that governs everything.

| | Commodity note (§13) | Service credit |
|---|---|---|
| Backed by | Matter in a shed | **A future hour of a living person** |
| Survives issuer collapse | Yes — grain is still there | **No** |
| Can be inspected | Yes, walk in and look | No — it does not exist yet |
| Can walk away | No | **Yes — practitioners emigrate** |
| Fungible | Yes, 1 kg = 1 kg | Only within a skill and quality band |
| Storable | Yes | **No — an unworked hour is gone forever** |

The whole reason the commodity design is robust is that **the backing
survives the institution.** If the cooperative collapses, the maize is still
in the warehouse and it is still yours. A service credit has no such
property. It is a claim on someone's future willingness and ability to work.

### The backing can emigrate

Modelled: a pool of 4,000 outstanding credit-hours backed by three doctors.

| Event | Coverage | Holders lose |
|---|---|---|
| Start (3 doctors) | 1.20× | — |
| 1 doctor leaves | 0.80× | **20%** |
| 2 leave | 0.40× | **60%** |
| 3 leave | 0.00× | **100%** |

Health-worker emigration from low-income countries is not hypothetical; it is
one of the defining features of the sector. **A currency backed by scarce
professionals is backed by exactly the people most likely to leave.**

Grain cannot get on a plane.

---

## 15.3 Why doctor-hours cannot clear

A credit must be redeemable or it is a queue ticket. Modelling supply against
demand:

| Service | Ratio | Demand covered | Clears? | Backlog |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Doctor (typical low-income ratio) | 1:25,000 | **6.4%** | No | 15.6 years |
| Doctor (generous) | 1:5,000 | 32% | No | 3.1 years |
| Nurse / clinical officer | 1:2,000 | 80% | No | 1.25 years |
| **Community health worker** | **1:500** | **320%** | **Yes** | 0.31 years |
| Teacher | 1:60 | 67% | No | 1.5 years |

**At realistic ratios, doctor-hour credits are unredeemable by construction.**
Issue them and you have created a claim on a resource that does not exist —
which is precisely the fiat-promise failure this whole blueprint was built to
escape. It would be worse than a bad currency; it would be a *lottery ticket
for care*, and the people holding the most would be the people who could
afford to accumulate them.

The pattern is clean and it is the central design finding:

> **Service credits work where the skill is abundant. They fail where it is
> scarce. And the sense of injustice is strongest exactly where they fail.**

---

## 15.4 The Fureai Kippu trap: the sick cannot earn

Japan ran this experiment for decades. *Fureai Kippu* — "caring relationship
tickets" — let people earn credits by caring for the elderly, bankable for
their own future care. It is the closest real-world precedent to your idea.

**Its documented central failure:** service users were often unable to earn
credits *because of their frailty and disability*, and so ended up buying
services with cash instead. User fees grew to as much as **70% of scheme
income** in some organisations — a barrier for exactly the low-income older
users the system existed to serve.

Modelled on a 1,000-person community:

| Segment | Can earn | Needs | Self-sufficiency | Needs subsidy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Healthy adults | 66,500 h | 4,200 h | 15.8× | No |
| Frail elderly | 2,250 h | 4,500 h | **0.50×** | **Yes** |
| Chronically ill / disabled | 2,000 h | 4,000 h | **0.50×** | **Yes** |
| Children | 250 h | 750 h | **0.33×** | **Yes** |

**Earning capacity is inversely related to need.** This is not a bug to be
patched — it is the definition of illness. A currency whose unit is labour
systematically underserves everyone who cannot labour, which is the same
population that needs care most.

A pure service currency therefore **reproduces the injustice it was built to
fix**: the sick pay more, in a harder-to-obtain unit, exactly when they can
least produce.

**Any care-credit design must include a grant channel — credits issued to the
sick, not earned by them — funded from somewhere other than the sick.** In
this blueprint that means the gate levies (§7.2).

## 15.5 Flat rate versus skill premium

Time banking's founding principle is that **one hour equals one hour**, a
lawyer's or a cleaner's alike. It is morally attractive and it is the point
of the design.

But run the arithmetic on a seven-year training and a thirty-year career:

| Skill multiplier | Lifetime credits if trained | If untrained | Net | Rational? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1.0 (flat)** | 48,000 | 59,200 | **−11,200** | **No** |
| 1.5 | 72,000 | 59,200 | +12,800 | Yes |
| 2.0 | 96,000 | 59,200 | +36,800 | Yes |

Under a flat rate, training **costs you 11,200 credit-hours over a lifetime**
— you spent seven years not earning and are then paid identically to someone
who spent those years working. Nobody trains as a surgeon under those terms
except from vocation, and a health system cannot be staffed on vocation
alone.

**The breakeven premium is only 1.23×.**

That number matters more than it looks. Market wage differentials between a
doctor and an unskilled worker are frequently **10–50×**. The premium
actually required to make training rational is **about 23%.**

> **The economic function of a skill premium is fully served at ~1.25×.
> Everything above that is not incentive — it is rent, captured because the
> patient has no alternative and cannot postpone.**

This is the precise, defensible version of your grievance, and it is stronger
than "doctors are overpaid." It says: *a modest premium is necessary and
just; the 30× premium is unrelated to the incentive it claims to justify.*

**Recommended: cap skill multipliers at 1.5×**, with training stipends (§14.7)
carrying the rest — which also lowers the required premium further, since the
trainee is not going unpaid for seven years.

## 15.6 What to actually build

Not a doctor-hour currency. A **three-instrument system**, each matched to
what it can actually deliver.

### Instrument 1 — Service credits, for abundant labour only

| Eligible | Not eligible |
|---|---|
| Community health work | Surgery |
| Childcare, elder companionship | Specialist diagnosis |
| Teaching, literacy, numeracy | Anaesthesia |
| Construction labour, repairs | Obstetric emergency care |
| Water carrying, farm labour | Anything with a life-safety scope limit |

**Rule: a service is creditable only if the ratio of providers to population
lets the credit clear within 90 days.** Anything scarcer gets a different
instrument.

Credits carry demurrage like Tier A notes — an unworked hour is gone forever,
so a stored hour should not hold value indefinitely. This also stops
credit accumulation becoming a claim on other people's future labour.

### Instrument 2 — A health fund, not a currency

For scarce care, the correct instrument is **risk-pooling, not exchange**.
This is not a retreat into conventional finance; it follows from the
arithmetic above.

```
   Everyone contributes a little, always
   (gate levies §7.2 + small commodity contributions)
                    │
                    ▼
            COMMUNITY HEALTH FUND
         holds grain, iron, and some FX
                    │
        ┌───────────┴───────────┐
        ▼                       ▼
  pays practitioners      buys consumables
  in RCU + credits        in hard currency
        │                       │
        └──────► CARE ◄─────────┘
              free at use
```

**Why pooling and not credits:** illness is random and lumpy. A credit system
makes you pay when you are sick. A fund makes everyone pay a little when they
are well. **The entire purpose is to break the link between "I need care" and
"I must now find payment"** — which is the exact mechanism that turns illness
into destitution.

The fund can hold RCU commodity notes, which is what makes this work without
shillings: the community's grain and iron become the reserve that pays the
clinic.

### Instrument 3 — Training bonds

Since scarce practitioners cannot back a currency, **create more of them.**

The community funds an apprentice's training (§14.6) in RCU; the graduate
repays in **service-hours to the community**, not in cash. This converts an
unbackable claim on a scarce person into a backable investment in making them
less scarce.

| | Value |
|---|---|
| Community funds | Stipend + master's fee, in RCU |
| Graduate repays | Committed service-hours over 3–5 years |
| Enforcement | Social and contractual, not debt bondage |
| Failure mode | Graduate emigrates — mitigate by paying well *and* in something spendable locally |

**Explicit caution:** a service-hour obligation must never become
indenture. Cap the total, allow buy-out in commodity units, and make the
obligation lapse on illness or disability.

## 15.7 Prior art you should know

Your idea has been tried, repeatedly, and the results are instructive rather
than discouraging.

| System | What it shows |
|---|---|
| **Time banking** (Cahn, 1980) | Hundreds of schemes across dozens of countries. Works for care, tutoring, errands. Explicitly struggles with "highly specialised and technical" services such as medical procedures — providers cannot find matching value |
| **Fureai Kippu** (Japan, 1973–) | Largest care-credit system ever run. Frail users could not earn; cash fees became up to 70% of income; growth stalled after public long-term care insurance arrived |
| **LETS** (Linton, 1983) | Mobilises latent skills where cash is scarce. Chronic problem: credit accumulation without matching demand |

Two findings recur across all of them and both apply here:

1. **People earn credits more easily than they spend them.** In early time
   banks, nobody spent more than ~20% of what they earned. Accumulated
   unspendable credits are demoralising and eventually kill participation.
   *Mitigation: demurrage, plus guaranteed acceptance for something people
   actually want.*
2. **Specialised services break the model.** This is the same finding as
   §15.3, reached independently by practitioners rather than by arithmetic.

**Fureai Kippu also found something genuinely encouraging:** elderly
recipients often *preferred* carers paid in credits over those paid in money,
because the relationship felt different. For abundant care work, service
credits are not merely a workaround — they may be better than cash.

## 15.8 Integration with RCU

| Mechanism | Effect |
|---|---|
| Service credits as a Tier A class | Demurrage-bearing, non-storable, abundant-labour only |
| Health fund holds RCU notes | Grain and iron become the health reserve — no shillings needed |
| Gate levies fund care and training (§7.2) | Gives the levy its most popular possible purpose |
| Training bonds repaid in service-hours | Converts unbackable claims into more practitioners |
| Practitioners paid in RCU + credits | Answers "we cannot afford a doctor" without cash |
| FX carve-out for consumables | **Unavoidable.** ~25% of care cost must be bought abroad |

### The honest limit

**Roughly 40% of the cost of care cannot be met by any local currency.**
Consumables, drugs and equipment come from outside and must be paid for with
exports or hard currency. A community can pay its people in RCU. It cannot
pay a Chinese glove manufacturer in maize claims.

This mirrors §0's standing admission that RCU cannot back imports. Health is
where that limit bites hardest and most visibly.

## 15.9 New risks

| Risk | Score | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Credits issued against scarce specialists; cannot clear** | **20** | Clearing test: no credit unless redeemable in 90 days |
| **Sick cannot earn; system reproduces the injustice** | **20** | Grant channel funded from gate levies, not from patients |
| Flat rate destroys training incentive | 15 | Skill premium capped at 1.5×; stipends carry the rest |
| Practitioner emigration wipes out credit holders | 16 | Never back credits on <5 practitioners; diversify; insure |
| Service-hour obligations become indenture | **15** | Hard caps, commodity buy-out, lapse on disability |
| Credits accumulate unspent | 12 | Demurrage; guaranteed acceptance |
| Under-qualified practice to meet credit demand | **16** | §14.6 scope-of-practice limits are absolute |

## 15.10 Summary

**Your grievance is substantially correct.** Illness bankrupts families while
providers are insulated; equipment at ~8% of cost is not a sufficient
explanation; and the market skill premium of 10–50× is far above the ~1.25×
that training actually requires. Everything above that is rent extracted from
people who cannot postpone or shop around.

**Your proposed solution is right for part of the problem.** Service credits
genuinely work for abundant labour — care, teaching, construction, farm work
— and Japanese experience suggests care given for credits can even be valued
more highly than care given for cash.

**But it cannot work for scarce specialists**, for three independent reasons,
any one of which is fatal:

1. **It cannot clear.** One doctor per 25,000 people covers 6.4% of demand;
   the credit is a 15-year queue ticket.
2. **The sick cannot earn.** Need is inversely related to earning capacity —
   Japan proved this over decades.
3. **The backing walks away.** Grain cannot emigrate. Doctors can, and do.

**What to build instead:** service credits for abundant labour, a
commodity-backed health *fund* for scarce care, and training bonds to make
the scarce less scarce. The fund is the important one — because the mechanism
that destroys families is not the *price* of care, it is being asked to pay
it **at the moment of illness.** Pooling breaks that link. A credit system
does not.
