# 14. People: recruitment, trust and skills

The other thirteen sections model grain, iron, decay and ledgers. This one
models the constraint that binds first and hardest: **the people who have to
run it.**

It is written as a correction. The source material for this section — a "5%
Field Manual" — contains several genuinely excellent operational ideas and
one doctrine that would **destroy the currency project if implemented**. Both
are treated here at full strength.

---

## 14.1 What the manual gets right

These should be adopted, and three of them are already load-bearing
elsewhere in this blueprint.

| Principle | Why it is correct | Where it already appears |
|---|---|---|
| **Judge by action, not speech** | Past behaviour is the best available predictor. Cheap talk is cheap | §6.4 inspector variance statistics |
| **Start with one person, grow by referral** | Trust transfers along existing ties; cold recruitment does not build it | §9.2 anchor acceptors |
| **Test with small commitments first** | Graduated exposure limits the damage of a wrong judgement | §3.2 loss waterfall |
| **Do something concrete early** | A completed small project proves capability better than any plan | Phase 0 exit gates |
| **Disloyalty is a system diagnostic, not a moral event** | **The strongest idea in the manual.** See §14.5 | New — now doctrine |
| **Apprenticeship over credentialism** | Skills transfer by supervised practice; this is how every trade worked for millennia | New — see §14.6 |
| **Elders hold undocumented knowledge that dies with them** | Correct and urgent | New — see §14.6 |

The disloyalty-as-diagnostic principle is genuinely valuable and is adopted
wholesale in §14.5. It converts betrayal from a catastrophe into information,
which is exactly the right posture for a system that will certainly
experience both.

## 14.2 The doctrine that would kill this project

> *"Ignore the 95%. Do not attend large public meetings. Do not share plans.
> Keep the core at 10–15."*

**For a preparedness cell this is defensible. For a currency it is fatal**,
and the reason is arithmetic rather than ideological.

A currency is worth something only because **other people accept it**. Its
value is a direct function of how many people are in the network. This is the
one asset class where being small is not a security posture — it is
worthlessness.

### The numbers

The blueprint's own Phase 1 kill criterion (§9.4) requires **≥60% of traders
in the pilot market accepting RCU**. Against a 10–15 person cadre:

| Market | Adults | Traders | Accepting traders needed | Cadre of 15 covers | Shortfall |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Small village | 800 | 60 | 36 | **42%** | 21 |
| Large village | 3,000 | 240 | 144 | **10%** | 129 |
| Small town | 12,000 | 900 | 540 | **3%** | 525 |

**A 15-person cadre is only 60% of the traders in a market of about 312
adults** — a hamlet too small to sustain a warehouse, an inspector rotation,
or a diversified commodity base.

The conclusion is unavoidable: **the cadre cannot be the network. It can only
be the thing that builds the network.** Those are different jobs, and
conflating them is the manual's central error.

### The three other collisions

| Manual doctrine | Blueprint requirement | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| "Do not share sensitive plans" | §6 public member roll, published inspector variance, externally-held checkpoints | **Direct conflict.** Secrecy here means unauditable, which means untrustworthy |
| "Do not attend large public meetings" | §7.4 quarterly public assembly reading out the community fund accounts | **Direct conflict.** The fund's legitimacy *is* its publicity |
| "Grow quietly, avoid notice" | §8.5 proactive central bank engagement in Year 2 | Already rejected. Wörgl was suppressed *because it succeeded*, at 12,000 schillings |

**A warehouse cannot be clandestine.** It is a building full of grain that
people must walk to. A currency cannot be secret; it must be recognised on
sight by strangers. The security model for this project is not concealment —
it is **legitimacy, transparency and legal standing.**

### The correct structure

```
   CORE CADRE (10-15)          the manual's model is right HERE
   deep trust, does the work,
   holds keys, takes the risk
            │
            │  builds and serves
            ▼
   ACCEPTOR NETWORK (100s)     the currency lives HERE
   traders who accept RCU,
   need reliability not loyalty
            │
            ▼
   USER BASE (1000s)           the point of the whole exercise
   anyone who holds a note
```

Dunbar's layers are real: roughly 5 in a support clique, 15 in a sympathy
group, 150 in an active network. **Deep trust genuinely does cap out near
15.** The manual is right about that and wrong about the implication —
because *acceptance does not require deep trust.* A trader accepts a note
because it is verifiable and other people take it, not because she loves the
cadre. **The system is engineered precisely so that strangers can transact
without trusting each other.** That is what the cryptographic signature, the
warehouse inspection and the redemption right are all *for*.

Requiring deep trust from every participant would discard the entire
technical apparatus of this blueprint.

## 14.3 The 80/15/5 rule: useful heuristic, false as anthropology

**Useful:** contribution to any voluntary effort is steeply unequal. A small
minority does most of the sustained work. Every organiser discovers this, and
planning for it is realistic.

**False:** the claim that this reflects fixed human types, and the prediction
that follows from it.

> *"In a crisis, the 80% will panic, freeze, or flee."*

This is the single most confidently stated claim in the manual, and it is
**contradicted by roughly seventy years of disaster research.** E.L.
Quarantelli and the Disaster Research Center reviewed hundreds of field
studies and found that panic is *rare*; the normal response to catastrophe is
**prosocial** — cooperation, mutual aid, and the spontaneous formation of new
groups to meet needs. Crime often falls. Strangers help strangers. Kathleen
Tierney's work on Hurricane Katrina documents the opposite failure: officials
acting on imagined public disorder while communities were in fact
self-organising. The phenomenon has a name in the literature — **elite
panic** — and it describes decision-makers, not the public.

The manual invokes the Siege of Sarajevo as proof that communities must
control access and exclude outsiders to survive. **The historical record says
close to the reverse.** Sarajevo survived on improvised, inclusive mutual aid:
neighbours of different ethnicities pooling stoves and fuel, whole apartment
blocks allocating tasks, communal kitchens, people risking sniper fire to
carry water for the elderly. *La Benevolencija*, the Jewish community
organisation, ran a public kitchen, a pharmacy and clinics **explicitly
serving everyone regardless of ethnicity**, and became one of the city's
lifelines. Solidarity was the survival mechanism, not the luxury.

### Why this matters operationally, not just factually

If you believe the 80% will turn feral, you will:

- build for defence against your neighbours instead of cooperation with them
- exclude people whose participation the currency actually requires
- treat the acceptance network as a threat rather than as the product
- and, in the moment when spontaneous mutual aid appears, **fail to recognise
  and use it**

A currency built by people who expect their neighbours to be predators will
be designed as a fortress. **Fortresses do not circulate.**

**Retain** the 80/15/5 rule as a planning heuristic for *who does unpaid
organising work*. **Discard** it as a prediction about behaviour under
stress, and discard the fatalism entirely: the 80% are not a write-off, they
are the acceptance base without which none of this functions.

## 14.4 Screening: the test is an instrument, and instruments have error

The manual's small-commitment test — *"bring 5 kg of rice on Saturday"* — is
a good idea. But it is a diagnostic instrument, and every instrument has a
false-negative rate that must be reasoned about.

### The bias, quantified

Same population size, **same underlying rate of reliability (5%)**, differing
only in material circumstances. "Miss rate" is the chance a genuinely
reliable person fails for reasons unrelated to character — no bus fare, a
sick child, piecework that day, no phone credit:

| Candidate pool | Miss rate | Reliable people found | Recall | Reliable people wrongly rejected |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Comfortable | 5% | 47 of 50 | **94%** | 3 |
| Destitute | 45% | 27 of 50 | **54%** | **23** |

**The same test discards twenty additional reliable people purely because
they are poor.** It is not measuring commitment. It is measuring slack —
the spare cash, time and predictability that poverty removes.

This matters enormously here, because the people the currency is *for* are
the ones with the least slack. A screening process tuned for the comfortable
systematically selects the comfortable, and then the project reports that
poor people were "unreliable."

### The base-rate problem

A second, subtler error. With a 5% base rate and a 10% false-pass rate, of
everyone who passes a single test **fewer than 40% are genuinely reliable.**
Most passers are false positives, simply because unreliable people vastly
outnumber reliable ones.

The manual's own remedy is correct and should be stated as the reason:
**three to five independent observations, not one test.** Repeated
independent tests raise precision sharply. That is the statistical
justification for graduated trust, and it is stronger than the intuition.

### Design rules that follow

1. **Make tests cost-free to attempt.** Provide transport money. Hold
   meetings where people already are, at times that do not compete with paid
   work. A test that costs a day's wage measures wealth.
2. **Offer several ways to pass.** Labour, information, introductions,
   childcare, local knowledge — not only cash or goods.
3. **Ask why before concluding.** A missed commitment is a data point about
   circumstances until you have checked which it is.
4. **Never use a single test as a gate.** Precision is too low.
5. **Test for the specific role.** A warehouse guard needs different
   reliability than a note verifier. Screen for the job, not for virtue.

## 14.5 Disloyalty as diagnostic — adopted as doctrine

This is the manual's best idea and it is adopted without qualification. When
something goes wrong, the operative question is not *"who is the bad
person?"* but **"what did this reveal about our system?"**

| Event | Wrong question | Right question | Structural fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grain missing from store | Who stole it? | Why did one person have unsupervised access? | Two-person rule; split caches; daily count |
| Plans reached an official | Who talked? | Why did that many people know? | Compartmentalise; reduce need-to-know |
| Member stopped showing up | Why are they lazy? | What changed in their life, and did we notice? | Regular welfare check-ins |
| Member took extra rations | How do we punish this? | Did our distribution fail to reach an acute need? | Emergency allocation with no shame attached |
| Inspector over-graded | Is he corrupt? | Was he paid enough, rotated, and monitored? | §6.4 controls |

**The engineering justification.** This blueprint already assumes a fraud
rate and funds an insurance pool for it (§5.5), already budgets 2% shrinkage
as within tolerance (§3.2), and already treats the redemption gate as a
normal instrument rather than an emergency. Treating human failure the same
way is consistent, not soft: **the system is designed to absorb a known rate
of loss and learn from each instance.**

A group that treats every failure as betrayal will hide failures. Hidden
failures compound silently and surface all at once. That is precisely how a
warehouse discovers a 40% shortfall instead of a 4% one.

**One limit, stated plainly.** Diagnostic framing is not infinite tolerance.
Repeated diversion after needs have been met, or coordinated sabotage, is a
different category and calls for removal from sensitive roles under §6.5.
The distinction is *whether the underlying cause has been addressed*. Fix the
system once; if the behaviour persists against a fixed system, it is about
the person.

## 14.6 Apprenticeship: right idea, wrong arithmetic

Training young people under existing elders is the correct model, for the
reason the manual gives: **it is how skills have transferred for most of
human history**, and it costs no tuition. Germany and Switzerland still run
formal dual-training systems with strong outcomes. The knowledge-preservation
argument is also urgent and correct — undocumented expertise dies with the
people who hold it.

### But the growth claim is not survivable

The manual projects 1 doctor becoming 20 in a decade. Modelled with honest
attrition — 60% completion, 70% retention in the community, 8% annual loss of
elderly masters, and a new graduate needing 3 years of practice before
supervising anyone:

| Year | Masters left | Supervisors | In training | Practitioners |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 11.0 | 11.0 | 33 | 0 |
| 5 | 7.9 | 7.9 | 33 | **13.9** |
| 10 | 5.2 | 19.1 | 60 | **23.1** |
| 15 | 3.4 | 42.6 | 128 | **46.6** |
| 20 | 2.3 | 75.7 | 227 | **100.0** |

**Roughly 1.9× in ten years, not 20×.** The binding constraint is not
enthusiasm and not the supply of willing apprentices — it is **supervision
capacity**, which shrinks as masters age out and only recovers once early
graduates mature into teachers.

### The honest success criterion

Not "20× in a decade." The real and entirely sufficient win:

> **The community holds more qualified practitioners than it started with,
> before the original masters are gone.** The model puts this at about
> **year 5**.

That is knowledge surviving its holders — which, as the manual correctly
argues, is what collapsed civilisations failed to do. It is a serious
achievement and it does not need inflating.

### What actually moves the numbers

| Lever | Effect | Practical action |
|---|---|---|
| **Retention** | Largest single effect | A graduate who migrates adds nothing locally. Make staying viable — pay them in RCU (§14.7) |
| **Completion** | Large | Four years unpaid is impossible for a poor household. Stipend from year one |
| **Supervision capacity** | Sets the ceiling | Recruit every retired practitioner; shorten time-to-teaching where safe |
| **Master attrition** | Silent killer | **Record everything now.** Filmed and written curricula survive a death |

### The safety limit

For construction, water and especially medicine, **a half-trained
practitioner is more dangerous than none.** Apprenticeship must have
scope-of-practice boundaries — what a year-2 apprentice may do unsupervised,
and what must always be referred. This is not bureaucratic caution; it is the
difference between a community health worker and a person who kills someone
with a misdiagnosis. Any real programme needs a qualified clinician to set
those boundaries and a written referral protocol.

## 14.7 How this connects to the currency

The human system and the monetary system are the same system, and each fixes
a problem in the other.

| Currency mechanism | People problem it solves |
|---|---|
| **Apprentice stipends paid in RCU** | Removes the "four unpaid years" barrier that excludes the poor — and the cooperative can pay in grain claims without holding shillings |
| **Masters paid in RCU for teaching** | Answers "experts won't work for free" using commodity claims, not cash the cooperative does not have |
| **Gate levies fund training** (§7.2) | Gives the community fund a visible, popular purpose, which is exactly what buys consent for the levy |
| **Skilled retention raises productive capacity** | More local production means more deposits, which means more currency backed by real goods |
| **Accepting RCU for cooperative fees** | The Wörgl mechanism — the issuer accepting its own currency is the strongest known demand anchor (§9.2) |

**The reinforcing loop:** training produces practitioners → practitioners
produce goods and services locally → goods back more currency → currency pays
for more training. This is the mechanism by which the currency stops being a
marketing scheme and becomes an economy.

### The counterpart risk

Paying stipends in RCU means **apprentices bear currency risk they did not
choose.** If RCU weakens, the trainee — among the poorest participants —
absorbs it. Mitigation: stipends denominated in **kg of staple grain** rather
than in units (which weight denomination in §13 makes natural), and a
guaranteed redemption window for stipend recipients ahead of the general
redemption queue.

## 14.8 Recruitment sequence for Phase 0

Replaces the manual's sequence, keeping its method and correcting its target.

| Step | Action | Test | Timeline |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Find **one** reliable person by observed action | Have they done something practical, unasked, in the last year? | Weeks 1–4 |
| 2 | Ask them who *they* trust; approach individually | Referral, not cold recruitment | Weeks 4–12 |
| 3 | Do one small concrete thing together | Who actually shows up | Weeks 8–16 |
| 4 | Build the cadre to 10–15 | 3–5 observations each, cost-free to attempt | Months 4–9 |
| 5 | **Cadre recruits the acceptor network** | ≥25 traders in writing | Months 6–12 |
| 6 | **Go public deliberately** — assemblies, WRRB, district | Legitimacy, not concealment | Months 9–12 |

Steps 5 and 6 are the corrections. The cadre is the engine; **the acceptor
network is the product.** A project that completes steps 1–4 brilliantly and
stops has built a preparedness group, not a currency.

### Who to prioritise, and why it differs from the manual

The manual prioritises hardship survivors and de-prioritises the comfortable.
Half right.

**Prioritise:** people already doing practical unpaid work; people trusted
across factional lines (essential for a currency, which must cross them);
traders with dense customer networks; elders with skills; people who have
survived hardship *and* remained oriented toward others.

**Do not exclude the comfortable.** The blueprint needs a shopkeeper with
storage, a teacher who can run numeracy training, a nurse, someone who can
talk to the district authority. §9.3 already identifies the **stigma trap** —
if RCU becomes "money for people too poor to have shillings," acceptance
collapses among the merchants who make it useful. Recruiting prosperous
early participants is a stated mitigation.

**The manual's instinct here is actively dangerous for this project.**

## 14.10 Summary

**Adopt:** action over words; referral growth; small graduated tests;
disloyalty as system diagnostic; apprenticeship; elder knowledge capture,
recorded now.

**Reject:** ignoring the 95%; secrecy as a security model; the claim that
people turn feral in crisis; single-test gatekeeping; 20×-in-a-decade
training projections.

**The one-line correction:** *the cadre is the engine, the network is the
product.* Build a core of 10–15 who can be trusted with keys and risk — then
spend most of your effort on the several hundred people who merely need to
find the currency **useful.** They do not need to trust you. That is the
entire point of building a system in which strangers can transact without
trusting each other.
